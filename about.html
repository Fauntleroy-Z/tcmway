Who Is Ollie?

Ollie is my pen name. The owl is part of the same idea: a curious, unpretentious guide who is more interested in helping you understand a tradition than in becoming a personality.

Behind the pen name is one real person. I am Chinese, I work in international trade during the day, and I have studied Chinese medicine independently for years. The text that changed my reading of the body was the Shanghan Lun (Treatise on Cold Damage, 《伤寒论》), especially its six-channel framework. I later passed China's national qualification examination for TCM pharmacist (执业中药师资格考试). That is a professional qualification in Chinese pharmacy, not a medical licence.

I am not a physician, and I do not practise medicine. I do not diagnose, prescribe, sell herbs, promise cures, or act as anyone's clinician. TCM Way is cultural education and careful observation, not medical care or a substitute for a qualified healthcare professional.

What TCM Way Is

Chinese medicine, culture, and everyday life — translated for the curious.

Chinese medicine does not sit in a sealed cabinet. It grows out of older ways of seeing the body, food, seasons, emotion, ritual, and daily life. TCM Way keeps the body at the centre while allowing those neighbouring worlds to come into view through four connected areas:

  • Body & TCM: classical ideas, six-channel pattern differentiation, organs and networks, diagnosis, and what tradition can and cannot tell us.
  • Food & Seasons: seasonal rhythms, the Chinese kitchen, food nature, and ordinary eating.
  • Ideas & Practice: Confucian, Daoist, and Buddhist ideas, plus embodied practices such as Baduanjin, standing practice, and taiji.
  • Culture & Everyday Life: history, objects, misunderstandings, family life, and observations from a real Chinese life.

How I Work

I try to keep three layers visible: what a classical source actually says, what modern research has or has not shown, and what I have noticed as one reader of the tradition. Those are not interchangeable forms of proof.

I use sources, name uncertainty, and correct mistakes when I find them. I use AI tools to help research, organise, edit, and test drafts, but I do not treat an AI model as a fact source, medical reviewer, or author. The byline remains a person's responsibility, and the editorial process is explained in the Editorial Policy.

I do not manufacture patients, clinics, credentials, spiritual authority, or personal experiences. If an article needs a lived detail, it comes from a real memory or an approved real-life story. If it does not have one, it should say less, not invent more.

What You Will Find

TCM Way publishes long-form guides, cultural explanations, practical introductions, and shorter field notes. Articles are developed in small, deliberate batches rather than churned out to fill a daily quota. The aim is clarity without flattening the subject, warmth without pretending certainty, and a useful path from a first question to a deeper understanding.

If a health concern is urgent or persistent, please do not use this site as a diagnostic tool. Seek medical care. The medical disclaimer applies to everything I write here.

If you want a slower stream of notes, sources, and new essays, you can join Letters from TCM Way. Replies and thoughtful questions are welcome.
