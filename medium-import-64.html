Shen, Hun, Po — TCM's Map of the Mind

COMICMARKER64

Last Tuesday, a meeting ran forty minutes over, and by the end you couldn't name a single thing anyone said. Your body was there. Your mind? Somewhere near Thursday's deadline. Traditional Chinese Medicine charted that split over two thousand years ago. TCM didn't lock the mind in one place. It divided mental life into distinct spirits and housed them across the body—shen in the heart, hun in the liver, po in the lungs. Heart, blood, breath: three seats in the living body, each holding a piece of who you are.

Long before brain scans, the practitioners who drew this map worked from pulse, tongue, and centuries of careful watching at the bedside.

The Architecture

The Huangdi Neijing laid the foundation. Suwen chapter 23 names the five stores: shen in the heart, po in the lungs, hun in the liver. The Lingshu's eighth chapter, Ben Shen (本神), sharpens the picture. Life begins with jing, the essence inherited from our parents. When the two essences unite, what arises is called shen. What follows shen back and forth, drifting into abstract thought, is called hun. Po clings to the physical side: essence, flesh, reflex. The full set runs to five: the spleen stores yi and the kidneys store zhi; heart, liver, and lungs hold the three that shape sleep and waking.

When you wake at 3 a.m., this map tells you which part of your mind is loud—and what the old tradition reached for.

Shen: The Spirit That Decides

A practitioner reads shen in the eyes. An eye with shen has light behind it, tracking movement with steady focus; an eye without shen looks vacant. Clear awareness, presence, the capacity to inhabit the room you stand in: this is shen, and the heart is its home. When shen is unsettled, evenings dissolve into static.

Hun: The One Who Leaves at Night

Blood is the hun's resting place. Hun lives in the liver, moored in blood; when blood is plentiful, the hun has somewhere to settle. Hun is imagination, long-range vision, the part of the mind that wanders while the body rests. When activity slows at dusk, blood returns inward. The hun settles in. But when liver blood runs thin or heat disturbs that shelter, the hun cannot rest. In traditional practice, waking at 2:00 a.m.—the liver's hour—means the hun has drifted loose. The causes differ: heat scatters it—red tongue, thirst for cold drinks. Blood deficiency leaves nothing to anchor it—pale nails, a thready pulse. Qi stagnation (stress, a wiry pulse) or blood stasis (a dark, purplish tongue) can block the liver's storage. Same symptom, different root.

Po: The One Who Stays Home

A horn blares, and your spine snaps straight before intellect catches up. That is po: raw animal life, housed in the lungs and carried on the rhythm of Qi, belonging to flesh, reflex, and instantaneous response. It runs the body's machinery: swallowing, dilating pupils in the dark. Modern Mandarin's word for boldness, po li, means "po-strength"—but the connection is folk memory, not direct lineage.

The Kite and the String

The three work like a kite in the wind. The hun is the kite—light paper and bamboo, eager to catch drafts and climb. The po? Spool and twine, anchoring the frame. The shen decides when to haul it home.

What the Modern Maps Echo

Neuroscience found some of the same divisions. Christoff's 2016 review tracked the brain's default mode network—daydream, memory, the mind stepping away from input; Nir and Tononi showed REM sleep splitting vivid imagery from motion. That's the hun. Craig found visceral integration in the anterior insula—the body sensing itself. That's po.

Three Questions at 3 a.m.

It's 3:15 a.m., and the room is pitch black. You're awake. The old map has its own quiet triage: which traveler is talking loudest? Is it the hun, racing down future pathways: the garage, tomorrow's messages? Traditional practice gave the wanderer a boundary: a notebook by the bed, the thought on a single page, and a quiet line: "We deal with this at eight o'clock." Then the notebook closes and moves across the room. Is it the po, where your mind may be blank while your body trembles with raw static, chest tight, breathing shallow? Traditional practice reached for air: four counts in, six out, telling the po that no wolves are nearby. Is it the shen, where you feel a prickly dread, scanning the shadows? Folk practice answered through the senses: the weight of the heels, then objects named in the dark. Pillow, doorframe, clock, water glass, window, until shen is reminded the moment is safe. The body tells you which traveler is loud: hun runs storylines; po clenches the body before the mind speaks; shen keeps watch for threats. Remember: this bedside triage is an educational exercise from traditional concepts, not a clinical treatment for insomnia.

The same map runs on the night shift. I'm Ollie—jade pendant cold against my chest feathers, ear tufts testing the wind.

The jade has caught enough moonlight to glow faint green. My talons close on the branch. Pure animal reflex. Then I launch into the pitch black, my hun tracing invisible thermal drafts below. My wings cup the air, my head locks dead center, and everything goes motionless. That is shen. Just flight, focus, and dinner.

Some nights, all three speak at once. The old order handles them like unruly guests: breath first to calm the body, the notebook to trap the storyline, the naming to anchor the watcher. One at a time, until the room goes quiet.
