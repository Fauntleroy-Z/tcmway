Your Insomnia Has a Type — A TCM Field Guide

COMICMARKER65

The clock reads 2:14 a.m. My heart is a snare drum, and a grudge from 2019 is back in the room. I fell asleep fine. That is the insult.

I lie there rerunning it, jaw tight, shoulders near my ears. Not random: my body woke me on purpose — a particular hour, a particular feeling, the same pairing for weeks.

Insomnia is one word for a dozen different nights. Traditional Chinese medicine reads the hour you wake — and the way you wake — as a message with a type.

Each night points to a zheng, a pattern: how you look, when you wake, what you ate. Two come from the clock — 1 to 3, then 3 to 5; the rest by how you wake.

Type One: The Mind That Won't Switch Off

The evening vigil: forty-two tabs the moment your head hits the pillow. This belongs to the Heart and the shen — often to the Liver that stores the Blood. Suwen 10: "when a person lies down, the Blood returns to the Liver." When Liver Blood runs thin, the hun has no mattress, and the shen turns petty, recalculating your mortgage. Suan Zao Ren Tang, attributed to Zhang Zhongjing's Jin Gui Yao Lue, speaks to that Blood-deficient picture — a practitioner's prescription, not an online order. The traditional diet here: warm, early meals — dark greens, red dates. The tell is the falling, not the waking: can't get under is this one; surfacing at 1 a.m. is Type Two.

Type Two: The 1 to 3 a.m. Alarm

You fall asleep fine, then snap awake between 1 and 3 — alert, warm, annoyed, jaw clenched. The Liver governs smooth movement of Qi; 1 to 3 a.m. is its window. Three tongues, three nights: red with a yellow coat, stagnation turned to heat; thin and white, it hasn't; red and nearly bare, nothing left to move — empty heat rising off depleted Liver Yin and Blood. Your pulse is the one sign you can't take yourself; note the hour and the tongue, and bring both to the table. The old answer: an early dinner, no wine on work nights, the worries on paper.

Type Three: The 3 to 5 a.m. Club

The 3 to 5 waking is cool and strange: shallow breath, a constricted chest, a house too cold, too quiet. This hour belongs to the Lungs and the po, the corporeal soul. Grief disperses Qi — the Suwen's 悲则气消 — and dispersed Qi doesn't descend. Nothing heroic helps — grief wants warmth more than discipline: a hot dinner, one real call, out the door before dusk. The letter stays on the shelf, written and never sent.

Type Four: The Watchman Who Startles

A creaking floorboard, a rattling screen, and the heart leaps. Vigilance disguised as rest: dreams at full volume, waking as if you'd spent eight hours on perimeter duty around an empty warehouse. Heart-Gallbladder Qi deficiency. This wakefulness is reactive — sounds, not rumination. The old answer: reserves over vigilance — warm meals, early nights, less caffeine after noon.

Type Five: Slept Eight Hours, Woke Wrecked

Lights out at 10:30, awake at 7, wrecked anyway — you slept, and the sleep did nothing. Head full of wet cotton, limbs leaden, tongue pale and greasy. Spleen and dampness: too sluggish a core for heavy, late, or dairy-rich dinners. The old habit: earlier, lighter dinners.

Type Six: The Kitchen Still Running at Bedtime

The Suwen's seven characters: wei bu he ze wo bu an — when the stomach is not at peace, sleep is not either. Digestion needs Stomach Qi to descend; sleep needs defensive yang to withdraw inward. A stomach still churning a 9:15 dinner blocks that retreat. The old rule of thumb: the kitchen closes three hours before bed. No body locks its doors while the kitchen is still running. Dampness leaves you heavy but asleep; stomach discord keeps you awake.

Type Seven: The Night That Gives Nothing Back

You slept seven hours, maybe eight, and woke to a chest full of wet sand and a mouth tasting of bitter grease. That wrongness — heavy, greasy, sour — is phlegm-heat disturbing the Heart. The tongue tells: red, thick with a yellow coat; the pulse slippery and fast. Wen Dan Tang was set down by Chen Yan in 1174 on Sun Simiao's earlier formula. These formulas are practitioner-adjusted prescriptions, not shelf products — get the pattern backwards and the nights get worse. The traditional notes for this one: lighter evening meals, less late alcohol.

Where the Map Ends

All of it reads as one failed handover: when Wei Qi (the defensive yang) won't enter the Yin, the eyes stay open. Two windows stay blank: 11 p.m. to 1 a.m. belongs to the Gallbladder; 5 to 7 a.m. to the Large Intestine. Patterns stack: a 1 a.m. Liver wake on a foggy morning is one night, not two.

This is vocabulary, not a diagnosis. The routine stays small: lights down by nine. If the same hour keeps waking you for a month, take it to a practitioner. Severe or months-long sleep disruption deserves a doctor; modern guidelines make CBT-I, not a pill, first-line care, and apnea, restless legs, thyroid disease, and depression all impersonate these patterns.

That 2:14 wake-up was Type Two; the jaw and the grudge were the tell. The log stays on the nightstand: the hour, the jaw, the tongue in the mirror — three weeks of that beats any story.
