Overthinking Is a Spleen Problem

COMICMARKER63

The clock ticks past midnight, and a passing remark from a morning meeting begins its tenth lap through your mind. You turn the phrasing over, testing each syllable against imagined reactions. Meanwhile, beneath your ribs, your stomach sits heavy and distended, as if dinner from six hours ago never left.

Chinese medicine looks at this midnight stall with a practical eye. The tradition tracks a direct line: unfinished thoughts settle into the digestive center, not the head.

The Spleen Is Not an Anatomical Filter

When modern biology talks about the spleen, it points to a fist-sized filter in the upper left abdomen that cleans blood cells and manages immune defenses. The traditional Chinese medicine Spleen is different: a physiological network that transforms food into Qi and blood and moves nourishment through the body.

Suwen chapter 8 calls the Stomach and Spleen the officials of the granary from which the five flavors emerge. This naming mismatch trips people up in two predictable ways. Some hear the word, picture a filter, and dismiss the model; others hear that worry affects the Spleen and panic about organ failure. One map tracks cellular anatomy, while the other maps functional patterns in a living body. Overthinking that injures the Spleen reads as mental fixation without irritability; irritability points to a Liver component, while palpitations and poor memory point toward the Heart-Spleen pair.

Thought Knots the Digestive Center

The core passage appears in chapter 39 of the Suwen. The passage describes si ze qi jie, meaning that thought knots the Qi: when attention fixes on a single problem, the spirit settles onto it, and the proper Qi stops circulating. The knot metaphor describes a mechanical restriction, not broken tissue. When string knots, the material stays intact; the movement through it stops. Left unchecked, the tradition reads the pattern as bloating after meals, an appetite that comes and goes, and a tiredness sleep does not fix.

At one in the morning, nothing unties six hours of looping by force; five slow breaths, four counts in and six out, are a slower rhythm the body can borrow.

Suwen chapter 5 pairs five emotional habits with internal systems, connecting pensiveness to the Spleen through si shang pi, meaning pensiveness injures the Spleen. The Lingshu's Ben Shen chapter defines the yi as what the heart remembers and ties the Spleen to the ying that houses it. Memory, focused thought, and digestion share the same functional territory. When worry loops for hours, that fixity draws resources away from the quiet work of digestion.

Two Maps of the Gut-Brain Line

Modern gastroenterology watches the same intersection through the enteric nervous system, a network of 200 million to 600 million neurons lining the digestive tract.

In a review in Nature Reviews Gastroenterology and Hepatology, L. Van Oudenhove and Q. Aziz showed that psychosocial factors play an intrinsic role in functional dyspepsia, with anxiety able to impair gastric accommodation, the stomach's ability to relax and receive a meal. A 2022 systematic review of 14 randomized controlled trials with 1,434 participants found that psychological interventions may reduce dyspeptic symptoms, with the clearest effects in structured psychotherapy. J. F. Brosschot and colleagues described through the perseverative cognition hypothesis how worry and rumination prolong stress responses long after a trigger ends.

An equals sign between the classical Spleen and the enteric nervous system does not survive careful reading. One maps function; the other tracks cells. Both can be used together without forcing a single theory.

Eating at the Desk

A lunch container sits open beside the keyboard while messages flash on screen. You chew quickly and type mid-bite, and the meal slips past without ever registering.

A review led by E. Robinson in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition found that distracted eating moderately increases immediate intake; the brain misses early sensory cues. The old rule is blunt: eat when you eat. One common way people keep it: give one of the day's meals full attention, phone in another room, no screens at the table.

Four Everyday Practices

A worry window: fifteen minutes with a notebook, where looping concerns get written down.

After a meal, a slow ten-minute walk; the tradition reads gentle upright movement as helping Qi descend.

The first few bites stay quiet, each one chewed before the next, setting an unhurried pace.

One longer exhale: five quiet breaths before eating or sleep, four counts in, six out.

The Formula Named Return the Spleen

In 1253, physician Yan Yonghe recorded Gui Pi Tang, or Return the Spleen Decoction, in the Ji Sheng Fang for overthinking that taxes the Heart and Spleen with forgetfulness and heart palpitations. During the Ming dynasty, physician Xue Ji later added two ingredients.

This record stands as history rather than a prescription. Herbal formulas can interact with medications and are not appropriate in pregnancy without professional guidance. In contemporary practice, formulas are individualized according to pattern differentiation.
