Take a fork. Twirl it in a bowl of spaghetti. Now try to pull it straight up. The noodles wind around the tines and hold on. The fork doesn't slip. It grips.

Now swap that fork for a hair-thin metal needle. Swap the spaghetti for the soft tissue under your skin. That grip is real physics. Back in two thousand one, someone finally measured it.

A researcher named Helene Langevin set up a simple pull test. Her team used a force gauge to measure how hard it was to pull a needle back out after rotating it in tissue. The numbers climbed as the needle turned. A sensation people had described for centuries showed up as a line on an instrument. That quiet surprise is where today starts.

Last time I promised we'd ask what actually happens when a needle goes in. Not the philosophy. The physics. How a thin piece of metal changes what your body does. Today we do exactly that. We start with the tug. Then we move to your body's own chemistry. We'll end inside a brain scanner, and then back out into the old two-thousand-year story. And all along, I'll be honest about what the lab hasn't proven.

Let's start with that tug. Helene Langevin wanted to know why a needle resists when you rotate it. She wasn't chasing mystery. Her team pushed needles into tissue, turned them, and used a force gauge to record how much effort it took to draw them back out. Not a big machine. Just a needle, a motor, and a sensitive gauge. The pullout force climbed as connective tissue wound around the metal. Collagen fibers coiled around the shaft, the way spaghetti winds around a fork. Pull, and you felt resistance. A feeling people had described for centuries now had a mechanical explanation. One piece of the picture. Not the whole thing.

The tissue they studied is called fascia. It doesn't stop at the edge of one muscle and start fresh at the next. It runs through the body in continuous sheets. It wraps around organs, muscles, and bones like packing material that never quite lets go. Fascia has an architecture. When a needle enters and turns, the collagen bundles twist. The fluid around them shifts. Each turn adds tension, like winding a loose thread around a spool. The tighter the collagen winds, the more force it takes to withdraw. This is measurable. Repeatable. Not mysterious once you see it under a microscope.

But here's the part we need to be careful about. Because fascia is continuous, a tug in one spot might send some mechanical pull nearby. The open questions are how far that pull travels, how reliably, and what it does once it gets there. Researchers are still working those out. It is not a demonstrated body-wide signaling network. And it is not another word for the meridians. The channel system is a separate, older framework with its own logic. One view is measured with force gauges and microscopes. The other was built from centuries of observation of symptoms, pulse, tongue, and response to needling. They might touch. They are not the same. Blurring the two does neither any favors. That's the honest line. The tug is real. The reach is unsettled.

Now let's move from mechanics to chemistry. This is the part that brings most people in. Pain relief. Needling appears to trigger the release of the body's own opioid-like chemicals. Endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins. Your body already makes these during exercise or stress. The runner's high. The dull relief after intense effort. They are part of your internal pharmacy. These chemicals don't exist by accident. Pain is not a simple wire. The brain can turn the volume up or down. Think of a signal coming up from the body. At the spinal cord and in the brain, the volume can be adjusted. Your own opioids are one of the dials. When you run hard, they rise. Pain feels further away. That's the runner's high. After effort, the dull relief comes from the same system.

In two thousand four, a researcher named Han Ji-Sheng published work on electroacupuncture. The key finding was about frequency. Different stimulation frequencies seem to favor different opioids. Low frequency leaned toward enkephalins. High frequency leaned toward dynorphins. That's specific. But keep it in its place. That work used animals and electrical stimulation. Electroacupuncture sends a small current through the needle. That's different from ordinary hand needling. So the frequency finding is a clue, not a map for humans. It does show that the body has more than one opioid route.

There's another clue. It comes from a drug called naloxone. Naloxone is used to reverse opioid overdoses because it blocks opioid receptors. It doesn't make someone feel good. It blocks. In some experimental studies, naloxone reduced the pain-relieving effect of needling. Not in every case. Not in every study. That partial blocking tells us something real. The body's own opioids do some of the work. Not all of it. The pharmacy is real, but it's only one shelf.

Now let's look at another shelf. A two thousand ten study in the journal Nature Neuroscience pointed to a molecule called adenosine. Your body uses adenosine for many things, including pain signaling. The study was in mice. The needle creates a tiny local stress. Cells release ATP. Enzymes break that ATP into adenosine. The adenosine then binds to A1 receptors. Those receptors act like a dimmer switch for pain signals. Turn them up, and the signal gets quieter. The researchers gave the mice a compound that boosts adenosine. The pain-relieving response got stronger. They bred mice without the relevant receptor. The effect disappeared. Clean result. For mice. Mouse and human biology overlap a lot at this level of cell signaling. But overlap isn't identity. Whether adenosine stars the same way in humans is still an open question. So say it plainly. A mouse study. A promising lead, not a finished story.

Now we go inside a brain scanner. Vitaly Napadow and his team have put people in fMRI machines. They watch what happens when a needle goes in. Their scans show something real. Needling shifts activity in brain networks that handle pain. But the scans don't tell one clean story. A study from nineteen ninety-eight, by Cho and colleagues, later became hotly debated. It reported that needling a point on the little toe lit up visual processing areas. The old tradition links that point to the eyes. Later studies could not always match that result. Another study found that needling a traditional pain point activated the limbic system. That's the brain's emotional center. It wasn't just sensory pain regions lighting up. Dozens of stories appear at once. So the honest word is heterogeneous. Different studies. Different points. Different people. Different results.

Here's where it gets even less clean. Sham needling also moves these networks. Sometimes a lot. A fake needle, or a real needle placed where the old theory says it shouldn't matter, can light up similar areas. Expectation alone changes brain activity. That's true for many things people believe are helping them. And here's a key limit. fMRI doesn't measure pain directly. It measures blood flow changes that track brain activity. When a region lights up, that region was active. But it doesn't tell you what the person felt. A person can have a brain shift and still say the pain didn't change. Or the pain can drop with no obvious scan change. So a shift on a scan and a real drop in pain are two different claims. Brain changes are interesting. They are not, by themselves, proof of clinical benefit. Mixing those two up is the exact shortcut we should avoid. This is not a magic light switch.

None of this is how acupuncture was described for most of its history. The older framework deserves time on its own terms. It came first. It still shapes how many people think about this practice. The text called Ling Shu [ling shoo] is traditionally seen as the acupuncture-focused half of the Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic. I can't read the original without help. But I can tell you the feel of it. It's not a quick recipe book. It's more like a manual for reading the body as weather. Qi can be stuck like humid air, thin like a dry wind, or sinking. The needle is one tool to adjust that.

The Ling Shu lays out a system built around Qi moving through channels. It describes nine classical needle types. Each one made for different depths and purposes. Some were for shallow pressing. Some for deeper drainage. That variety matters. It means the old system was not one needle for everything. The guiding idea is regulating Qi. Support it where it runs thin. Ease it where it's congested. And the approach depends on the person. Which channel, which pattern, which technique fits this moment. It's never one-size.

At the center of this is something called de qi [duh chee]. It means the arrival of Qi. Let's give that a shape. The person might feel heaviness, distension, a dull spreading ache, sometimes numbness. It's often described as a spreading or grabbing feeling. Not sharp. Not electric. More like a heavy pressure that moves slowly. Some people love it. Some find it strange. It can fade after a few seconds or linger a while. De qi is hard to define. It varies person to person. Some people feel it strongly. Others feel almost nothing. Not every tradition insists on it. The practice has its own internal disagreements about how much to seek it. That's okay. It's an old tradition, not a single agreed script.

The person holding the needle feels something too. A grip or resistance under the needle. That grip is not unlike the needle grasp Langevin measured decades later. Some studies link de qi to activity in certain nerve fibers. Faster A-delta fibers carry sharp, well-localized signals. Slower C fibers carry dull, aching ones. When you feel that heavy ache after needle insertion, those C fibers are likely active. That's a physical description. The traditional description uses different words. Both can be true at the same time. A nerve fiber response is one measurable thing a machine can register. Qi, in the old framework, is assessed through a much wider pattern. Tongue, pulse, symptoms, constitution. These are related observations. They are not interchangeable definitions of the same thing. The old view also says de qi is a sign that the needle has engaged the channel and that Qi is now moving. Without that arrival, the tradition holds, the work hasn't taken hold.

In the tradition's view, the approach shifts with the pattern. A deficiency picture might call for gentler needling and shorter retention. A stuck or excess picture might call for stronger stimulation to move it. The same point can be needled two ways. The effect, in the old framework, depends on the match.

The tradition also carries its stories. The Records of the Grand Historian tells of a healer named Bian Que reviving the crown prince of Guo. The prince had collapsed so deeply it mimicked death. The old text calls it corpse reversal. Everyone thought he was dead. Bian Que came, listened, felt, and said it was not true death. Then he used a needle. The prince revived. Later retellings compressed that revival into a single needle. Those retellings also connect it to a point at the crown of the head called Baihui. But the Records themselves do not name the point. Baihui is classically seen as a meeting place of the Yang channels at the crown. The tradition uses it to raise sunken Qi and restore consciousness. That's why later generations linked the story to that point. The image is vivid. One point, one needle, one breath back. Still, this is transmitted lore. It shaped how the tradition sees itself. It is not clinical evidence. Worth knowing. Worth holding loosely.

Now let's come back to the present, to a first session. It usually starts with questions. Not just about the pain. Sleep, digestion, stress, tongue, pulse. Someone trained in this tradition might press along the channels on your arms or legs. You might notice a tender spot you didn't know about. That can feel unexpected. The tongue gets a close look. The pulse is felt at both wrists. The questions might seem like a lot. That's the old diagnostic method. It doesn't just look at the symptom. It looks at the whole pattern.

After the needles go in, the room often gets quiet. You rest there for a while. Ten minutes, fifteen, maybe twenty. The lights might be low. Some people drift off. Others just breathe. The needles stay in place. They don't need to move. The old view says the body does the responding during that quiet time.

This is pattern differentiation. The person trained in the old framework looks at whether the pattern is more deficiency or more excess. But the real map is finer than that two-way split. Pain from Qi stagnation tends to move around. It worsens with stress and eases with pressure. It might come and go. It might ease when you move or press the area. Pain from Blood stasis stays fixed. You can point to one spot. It feels stabbing. It worsens at night when things are still. The old system sees these as distinct patterns. They need different needle techniques. Gentle needling to move Qi. Or firmer needling to break up stasis. The same point used two different ways can produce different sensations.

That's why a first session can seem strange. Someone comes in with shoulder pain. The questions turn to digestion. In the tradition, pain is rarely local-only. A shoulder problem might be read as a blockage along a channel that also runs through the gut. Digestion and circulation influence each other. So those unrelated questions do the real work. That's pattern thinking. The body is one connected system, not a list of separate parts.

Sterile single-use needles are standard. Most people feel a brief pinch on insertion. Then pressure or a dull ache once the needle settles. Some don't feel de qi at all. Risks exist, but they're uncommon with trained hands. Minor bruising or soreness is the most likely thing. Lightheadedness happens occasionally, especially the first time or on an empty stomach. Hygiene matters a lot. That's why training matters. Guesswork has no place here.

So here's the spine of it. When a needle goes in, the tissue grabs. That's collagen winding, measurable in a lab. The body appears to release its own pain chemicals. Opioids do some of the work. Not all. In mice, adenosine dials down pain signaling at the needle site. Brain scans shift, but sham needling and expectation shift them too. The old tradition saw all of this through a different lens. Channels, Qi, de qi. A needle arriving at the right place, in the right person, at the right moment. Two frameworks. Related observations, maybe. Not interchangeable.

Here's how I hold both of these. The tradition asks what this person's body is showing right now, and what helps. The lab asks what molecule just moved. Different tools. Different centuries. I can't prove they're describing the same thing. I can't prove they're not. Maybe the needle grabbing collagen is what the old texts called de qi. Maybe they're two separate things happening at once. Either way, people feel something real.

Here's what these sources do not establish. They don't give a blanket claim that acupuncture works for everything. They don't give an anatomical map of the channels, the way we have a map of blood vessels. They don't promise that a chemical change or a brain scan shift becomes a clinical result anyone would notice. And they don't reduce Qi to fascia, nerve firing, or endorphins. These are separate, partial pieces. Some come from a two-thousand-year-old observational tradition. Some come from a force gauge and a brain scanner. Treat both with respect. Treat neither as finished.

Remember the fork. The collagen winding is the physical echo of what the old texts called needle grasp. The lab calls it measurable resistance. The tradition calls it de qi. Maybe they align. Maybe they don't. But the image stays with me. Something thin, rotated, and held.
