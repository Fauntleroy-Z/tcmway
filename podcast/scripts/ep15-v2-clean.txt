Rio de Janeiro, two thousand sixteen. Michael Phelps steps onto the starting block at the Olympic aquatics center. The air is thick with chlorine and warm water. He pulls his cap down over his hair. He rolls his shoulders once, then again. The camera moves in close. His upper back is covered with dark purple circles. They run across his shoulders and shoulder blades in a loose pattern. Some are the size of small coins. A few are larger. Under the bright pool lights, they look almost black.

The image freezes for a moment. Then the race begins. But by then, the circles are everywhere online.

People who had never seen cupping filled social media with guesses. Some asked if he had fallen onto giant checkers, collided with blunt objects, or collected giant hickeys. The guesses got wilder and wilder.

Post after post circled the same thought. A back like that had to mean damage. The comments moved fast. Some compared the circles to checker pieces pressed into skin. Others guessed training equipment or heat lamps. A few joked about thick octopus arms. The guesses kept coming all night. But for a large part of the world, the image carried no alarm. It looked like someone's uncle after a long work week. It looked like a normal Tuesday.

For people who grew up around cupping, the same image was not a mystery. It was ordinary. It was just ba guan [bah gwahn]. Cupping.

The marks did not come from a blow. They came from suction. A cup had pulled the skin up, and the skin had answered with color.

That gap between the two reactions is what I want to unpack today. The same purple circle can look like an injury to one eye and a familiar relief to another.

Let me start with something simple. A cupping mark is a bruise-like mark. It usually comes from suction, not from a blow. The skin gets pulled upward, and the mark shows up after the skin is released. It looks like a bruise. It fades like a bruise. But the usual cause is different.

An ordinary bruise often comes from a bump or a hit. You knock your knee against a table, and later a dark spot appears. A cupping mark often comes from the opposite direction. The skin is lifted, not struck. That is the first thing to hold onto. Bruise-like marks, yes. A blow, no. The force comes from suction, and the marks are the skin's answer.

Now let me walk you through a session. Imagine a quiet room, a firm table, and a row of cups on a small tray. The cups can be glass, silicone, or plastic. The person doing the cupping places a cup against your skin. Then they create suction inside the cup. You feel the cup grab hold. The skin rises into the dome of the cup, like a little hill.

The cups are usually placed one at a time. The person doing the cupping may press a thumb into the muscle first. They are looking for the tight knot. Then they set the cup over that spot. Some people watch the skin rise. It looks like the cup has taken hold of a soft handful of skin.

That first cup is the strangest. You feel a firm pull. Not a blow, just a pull. Some people say it feels like a strong pinch that does not stop. Others say it feels like a finger pressing and lifting at the same time. The pull can be intense. If the suction is strong, it can be painful for a few seconds. Then the feeling settles. It becomes a steady pressure.

After a moment, you notice your breath. You may not have been breathing as deeply as you thought. The cup sits there. It asks your shoulder to be still. Some people watch the clock. Others close their eyes. The quiet ten minutes are part of it. The cup does not move. The body does the adjusting.

The cup stays in place for several minutes. You sit still or lie down. The room might be quiet except for a clock or soft music. You breathe. The pulling softens into something warm and strange. You know the cup is there, but you stop fighting it. Your body gets used to the lift.

Sometimes the timer is set for eight or ten minutes. Sometimes there is no timer at all. The person doing the cupping may come back and check the skin. They may loosen one cup and move it to another spot. Others prefer to leave everything in place. That still time is part of the ritual.

Then the cups come off. There is a soft pop, and a rush of cool air over the skin. The skin feels wide awake. Some people move their shoulders and notice the tight spot feels quieter. Some people feel warmth for an hour or two. Others feel it longer, or not at all. Many people compare it to a mild sunburn. Not painful, just present.

For some people, the warmth lingers. It is not a burn. It is a soft, heated feeling. You might touch the spot with your palm. It feels warmer than the skin around it. That warmth fades slowly. For some people, it is the best part.

The purple mark does not appear all at once. Over the next few hours, the color usually settles. You might look in the mirror and see a circle where the cup was. It looks dramatic. You might expect to feel sore. Instead, many people say the spot feels warm and sort of open. It can be tender if you press on it, and some people find it sore. Others feel only a light tenderness the next day. Everyone is different.

That next-day tenderness usually shows up when you press the circle. It does not throb on its own. You can go about your day. Lifting your arms might remind you the cup was there. Bending your back might feel a little stiff. It is a dull reminder, not a sharp alarm.

Now for the traditional side. In Chinese medical and folk usage, sha [shah] can refer to the redness or small spots that appear after techniques like gua sha or cupping. It is also a traditional concept, and its meaning shifts from place to place.

Traditional theory holds that cupping helps the body clear what feels stuck. The idea is that heat, sluggishness, and stagnant Qi can settle in one part of the body. Cupping is a way to bring that stuck feeling toward the surface. Then the body can let it go. When Qi and blood flow freely again, comfort returns. That is the tradition's view. It describes what people watch for. It is not a measured mechanism.

In the old way of thinking, the marks are not a problem. They are a sign that the body is doing its work. The cup has asked the skin to rise, and the skin has answered. The color is part of the process.

Many people grew up with this scene. In some families, the older person brings out the cups after work. A stool is set near the table. The cups are wiped clean. Then one by one, they are placed on the back. People chat while the cups sit in place. Tea is poured. A fan hums in the corner. No one rushes.

The same cups might have been used for ten or twenty years. They live in a kitchen drawer or a small wooden box. They smell faintly of glass and old smoke. The person who uses them may not call it a treatment. They just say, 'Come here, let me help you loosen up.' That sentence carries a lot of care.

After the cups come off, the marks are read like a familiar language. People look at the circles and talk about the body. Darker here, lighter there. You've been sitting too long. You've been carrying too much this week. It is not a formal test. It is a way to notice what the body has been holding. In some folk readings, darker marks are taken to mean more stagnation and lighter marks less, but the color scale varies by region and tradition. It is a conversation starter, not a verdict.

In traditional language, the cups are said to draw out wind, damp, and heat. The exact words depend on who is speaking. Some people say the cups open the surface. Others say they move what is stuck. The idea is always the same. The body carries too much. The cups ask it to let some go.

One family may call deep purple a sign of heavy tiredness, while another sees it as normal for that person's skin.

I want to be clear about that. The color talk is a folk tradition. It is not a formal reading. It is not a diagnosis. It is a comfortable way for people to talk about their bodies. It has been passed down in neighborhoods and families for a long time. But it is not a measurement you can take to a lab. It is part of the ritual.

Here is something that surprises a lot of people. Pulling skin with cups is not only Chinese. Old records from Egypt, Greece, the Arabic world, and Europe describe suction-based therapies. They were not one identical practice. Each place used its own tools and methods. But the core idea was similar. Suction on the skin, applied to a tight or tired spot.

Different traditions used different tools. Heated glass. Bamboo. Pottery. Metal. Animal horns with a small hole. The methods were not identical, and they changed over time. What they shared was suction on the skin.

Greek physicians like Hippocrates recommended it for body aches. In traditional Arabic healing, cupping also has a long history. In European folk medicine, cups made from horns or glass were used for ages. Different places used different tools, and the methods were not identical. But suction on the skin appears in many old traditions.

Old Egyptian papyri describe cups being placed over sore parts of the body. Greek texts speak of cupping as a common routine. Arabic practice has its own name and its own ceremony. European folk healers reached for a cup when a back was stiff. These were not copies of one another. They were separate discoveries of the same simple trick.

Records from different places describe similar practices, though they were not identical. A cup on the skin. A firm pull. A mark that fades. People in many cultures found something in that experience.

One important note. The kind of cupping I am talking about here is dry cupping. The skin is not intentionally cut or opened. Wet cupping is different. It involves letting blood. That is another practice with its own history and its own risks. Keep the two separate. When I talk about marks and sessions here, I am talking about dry cupping.

So do the cups work? That question depends on what you are hoping for. Many athletes and everyday gym-goers use cups on stiff backs and tight shoulders between training sessions. Some say the area feels looser for a while. Others say the warm ritual is the main benefit. Some people like the marks because the marks make them slow down. They see the color and remember to rest.

The science is limited and mixed. There is not one clean answer. Science likes clear measurements. Cupping gives a blurry picture. Some small trials suggest cupping may ease localized tension for a short time. Others find little difference from massage or a relaxing routine. One idea is that the strong skin signal may shift the body's attention. But that is just an idea. The reliable evidence for a meaningful benefit is still weak. The honest answer is that it is a wellness practice, not a cure. It will not fix an injury. If it helps, the help tends to be temporary. People return because it feels useful to them.

Most of the studies are small. Some compare cups to a fake treatment. Some compare cups to massage. The results do not line up into one clear answer. A few trials show a short-term easing of tight muscles. A few show no more than rest alone. That is why I keep calling it a wellness practice.

Some gym-goers say the tight band between their shoulder blades finally stops yelling after a cupping session. Others notice nothing beyond a warm back and a good nap later. The honest answer is that no one knows exactly why some people feel a clear change and others feel only a skin mark. That uncertainty is part of the tradition. People keep coming back because the ritual feels good, and the relief, when it comes, is real to them.

Now for safety. For many healthy adults, dry cupping is considered low risk when a trained person does it with clean tools. But it is not risk-free. The clearest risks are bruising, soreness, blistering, burns, and infection. It is not for everyone. Broken or infected skin, bleeding problems, blood thinners, and reduced skin sensation all need extra caution. Pregnancy, diabetes, significant anemia, immune suppression, and poor circulation are also situations that call for extra caution. That is why this is not a do-it-yourself practice.

Strong suction can leave a deep mark. That is why trained hands matter. No one should learn fire cupping from a video. The flame, the glass, and the skin all demand respect.

Open-flame fire cupping carries a real burn risk. The old fire method uses a small flame, and the flame is dangerous in untrained hands. It is never something to try without proper training. Even with training, burns can happen, and a burn from fire cupping can be serious. Clean tools and steady hands matter.

And if you see blistering, burns, or signs of infection after cupping, that needs proper care. Not home treatment. Not waiting to see. Proper care. Most simple marks settle on their own, though how long they last varies. Many fade within three to seven days. For some people, the marks may last a week or two or longer. The color may shift as the body clears them, but there is no fixed sequence. The skin returns to its normal look in time.

One thing I want to repeat. Cupping looks fierce. The purple circles are dark and graphic. But for a lot of people, the feeling is not fierce. The pull is strong, the mark is colorful, but the pain is often mild. Some people even fall asleep with the cups on. The visual is loud. The sensation is quieter.

That is the big difference from something like a fall or a fight. A hard impact can leave you sore for days. A cupping mark can look worse than it feels. The body has not been hit by a blow. The skin has been lifted by suction. Even so, suction is a physical force, and strong or prolonged cupping can cause bruising, blistering, or other skin reactions. In the tradition's view, the mark is a sign that the body is moving something out. In a modern view, it is a temporary skin change from suction.

Let me also say what cupping is not. It is not a fix for broken bones. It is not a cure for long-term pain. It is not a substitute for proper care when something is wrong. It is a wellness ritual. It has been around for centuries. It has a place in many cultures. But it is not magic. The marks are not badges of healing. They are marks.

That is why the Rio moment was so interesting. People saw the same circles and told two different stories. One story said injury. Another story said relief. The truth is somewhere in between. The marks are not wounds from a blow. They are surface marks from suction, and they can behave like a minor bruise. They are not proof of healing. They are a sign that suction has been used. The meaning people give them depends on where they grew up and what they were taught.

For Michael Phelps, the cups were part of his routine. For my grandfather's generation, the cups were part of the evening. The circles meant a tired back had been lifted. The circles meant someone had taken time to sit still and breathe. The circles meant the body had been asked to let go of something.

That is the part I want to leave with you. Cupping is old, simple, and a little strange. The cups pull. The skin rises. The marks come. Then the marks fade. No one can promise what you will feel. For some, it is relief. For others, it is only a warm back and a story.
