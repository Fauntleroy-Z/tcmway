I was walking through a park in late September a few years ago, and I noticed something that stopped me in my tracks.

A big oak tree was dropping its leaves. Not fighting it. Not hanging on. Just letting them go — one by one, drifting down in the golden light. And I remember thinking, wow, that tree looks so peaceful. So calm.

But my brain was doing the opposite. That week, I had bought a new planner. I had made a list of goals. I was pushing myself to be more productive after what I told myself had been a lazy summer. I was gearing up.

And then I see this oak, letting go of everything it had grown all year, and it hit me: the tree is not lazy. The tree is not failing. It’s doing exactly what autumn asks.

Hey, friends. This is East and Inner. I’m Ollie. Just me, talking to you.

Today I want to share something that might flip your whole idea of autumn upside down. We often think of fall as a season of fresh starts and pushing harder — back to school, back to work, end-of-year goals. But there’s an old Chinese medical text, over two thousand years old, that says something completely different. Autumn is not about starting up. It’s about slowing down. Not about accumulating. About letting go.

So why did I stop in my tracks that day? Because that oak showed me something the ancient text had already written. Let’s get into it.

The text is called the Huangdi Neijing, the Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon. In its second chapter, the Plain Questions, it gives a guide for living with each season. And when it gets to autumn, it doesn’t say get busy. Get organized. Push through.

No. It opens with a very soft, quiet phrase. The three months of autumn, it says, are called Rong Ping [rawng peeng].

Rong means to contain, to hold, to allow, to be at ease. Ping means level, calm, balanced, peaceful. Together, Rong Ping describes a state of calm containment. Settling. Leveling out. Imagine a lake after the wind dies down. The ripples soften, and the surface becomes smooth, like a mirror reflecting the sky. That’s Rong Ping.

Think about that. It’s not the energy of a fresh start. It’s the energy of a graceful conclusion.

And you know, every culture has a harvest festival. It’s like we all instinctively mark this season of accounting. In China, there’s the Mid-Autumn Festival, with the full moon and mooncakes, families gathering to celebrate abundance. In the West, Thanksgiving. Europe has its own harvest traditions. And Samhain, which eventually became Halloween. These festivals are anchors. They honor the turning of the year. They remind us to take stock — not just of the harvest, but of our own lives. The Neijing’s Rong Ping echoes that. It’s a call to settle and see what we’ve grown.

Think of it as closing the year’s ledger. You sit with the tallies. What grew, what ripened, what you’re deeply grateful for. You also note the heavy things you can finally lay down. The ancients understood: we need a ritual moment to mark completion. Harvest festivals aren’t just about food. They’re a collective exhale, a way to say, this cycle is done, and we are still here. Autumn invites us to do exactly that for our inner lives.

The Neijing then describes the Qi of the season. It says, Heaven’s Qi becomes sharp, and Earth’s Qi becomes clear. You know this feeling. The air gets a crisp, almost biting edge. And the light on a sunny fall day — it’s so clear, so bright. It feels like the world is coming into sharp focus before it softens into winter.

So, how are we supposed to live in this sharp, clear world? The text gives us some very practical advice, and it’s not what you might expect.

Go to bed early and rise early, in rhythm with the rooster. As the days get shorter, our sleep should follow. We turn inward, honoring the sun’s retreat.

Modern researchers call these seasonal affective patterns. Shorter days give us less bright morning light. That light shift can change melatonin and serotonin levels. These are the chemicals that control sleep and mood. When the Neijing tells us to rise with the rooster and sleep with the sun, it’s actually the oldest sleep hygiene advice on record. It’s about syncing our internal rhythms with natural light. Simple and wise.

Your body runs on the light cycle. It’s not a metaphor. It’s biology. The ancients noticed the pattern. Modern science confirmed the mechanism. And our culture still tells us to hustle harder in September.

And about our minds? That part of us that often revs up in autumn. The Neijing is direct about that too. Keep your spirit-mind calm and at peace. Gather and restrain your spirit-Qi. Think of the difference between a lamp and a bonfire. Summer is the bonfire. Autumn is the lamp. Both give light. But the lamp burns steady and low.

It’s describing movement. In summer, our energy expands outward. Parties, travel, long bright days, being out in the world. In autumn, the energy is no longer expanding. It’s contracting. Gathering. Moving inward and downward. And the whole point of this guidance, the text says, is to keep the autumn Qi balanced within ourselves.

If we fight this natural gathering and instead push for summer-level expansion — more goals, more speed, more hustle — we create an internal clash. The Neijing warns that this can injure the Lungs.

Now, why does the text link autumn to the Lungs and a quiet spirit? To get it, we have to step into a framework called the Five Elements. In this system, every season connects to an element, an organ system, an emotion, and more. Autumn corresponds to Metal.

The Lungs in Chinese medicine do more than just breathe. They’re considered a tender organ — delicate and easily affected. They’re the first line of defense against outside influences like wind, cold, and especially dryness. Dryness is a big theme in autumn, and the Lungs are very sensitive to it. That’s why in traditional practice, you hear about moistening foods and gentle hydration this time of year. The Lungs also command the Qi of the whole body. They take in the new — fresh air — and release the old. In every breath, we’re doing that.

And the emotion linked to Metal and the Lungs? Grief. Or sadness. Sadness in autumn isn’t a flaw. It’s the season’s deepest intelligence.

Now, right there, you might feel a little nervous. Grief? Sadness? We often think something is wrong if these feelings come up. But in this framework, it’s not that autumn makes you sad in a clinical way. It’s that autumn provides the perfect container for processing sadness. The season itself is an invitation.

A tree has to let its leaves go. It doesn’t grieve the leaves. It lets them fall, and that release conserves energy for the winter. In the same way, this season gives us a chance to let go of old sorrows, disappointments, attachments that no longer serve us. That’s the work of the Metal element. It’s about refinement, telling what is precious from what is no longer needed.

When we refuse to process that grief, we push it away. We try to stay positive and busy. But that’s like the tree holding onto dead leaves. From this perspective, that clogs things up internally. It can weaken the Lung Qi. So the text is pointing to a two-way street: autumn gives us space to grieve well, not endlessly, and that release protects the Lungs.

Grief is the natural emotion of letting go. When we try to stay positive and hustle through autumn, we’re telling our Lungs to hold onto all those dead leaves.

And that oak tree I saw? It wasn’t struggling. It wasn’t sad. It was just doing what it’s done for millions of years. As I often like to say, the forest isn’t sad when it drops its leaves. It’s getting ready. Autumn is the season of the great exhale. And we can exhale, too.

So how do we actually live this, right now, in our modern world? Accepting the spirit of Rong Ping doesn’t mean you quit your job and disappear into the woods. It’s about small, conscious shifts.

Let me share a few things that help me.

First, reclaim your evenings. The Neijing’s advice to go to bed early is a direct response to the fading light. The days are shorter, so instead of fighting the darkness with bright screens and late-night work, lean in. An hour before bed, dim the lights. Switch on a small lamp instead of the overhead light — the room loses its sharp edges, and the shadows hug the corners. Put the laptop away. Read something physical, a real book. Listen to quiet music. Let your energy begin its natural descent with the sun. This alone sends a signal to your whole system that it’s time to gather, not scatter.

Second, take what I call a harvest walk. Go outside, no headphones, not for exercise, but for observation. Feel that sharp Qi in the air, that clear light. The scent of dry leaves and a hint of woodsmoke follows you. As you walk, take a gentle inventory of your year so far. What have you harvested? What projects came to a natural end? What ideas have run their course? This isn’t about judging yourself. It’s just about acknowledging what is complete. Notice what you’re still carrying that maybe isn’t needed anymore. I once heard a simple line that I love: you can’t see the forest floor if you never let the leaves fall. This walk helps the leaves fall.

I tried it last autumn on a quiet Sunday. I left my phone at home and walked to a small park near my house. The light was slanting low, not harsh, kind of honey-colored. I wasn’t looking for anything in particular. But after a few minutes, I remembered a project from that summer. A small creative thing. I’d let it go because it had served its purpose. I realized I’d never really acknowledged its completion. It was the first time I’d finished something and honored the ending instead of rushing to the next thing. Right there on the path, with dry leaves under my feet, I let myself feel a little proud. And then I let it go. That felt good.

Third, try a small letting-go ritual. The Metal element is about discernment. You can mirror that physically. Clean out one drawer. Go through your closet and donate clothes you don’t wear. Delete old files from your computer. Or write down a worry or a lingering resentment on a piece of paper and, safely, burn it. The physical act of releasing sends a powerful message to your mind and spirit: I am making space. Afterward, open the empty drawer and glide your hand across the bottom — it feels smooth, cool, and full of new possibility.

And fourth, breathe with the Lungs. Since this is their season, give them a little attention. Find a few minutes each day to sit quietly and follow your breath. Inhale the cool, crisp air, and with every exhale, imagine you’re releasing something you no longer need to carry. A deadline, a tension in your shoulders, a grudge. Notice the slight chill at your nostrils and the gentle rise and fall of your ribs. This is the season of the exhale. Let it be long and slow.

Now, you might be wondering about food. Because in the TCM framework, diet shifts with the seasons, and autumn is all about countering dryness and supporting the Lungs. The advice is to eat moistening, nourishing foods that reflect the harvest. Pears are a classic — they help moisten the Lungs. Pumpkin, winter squash, sweet potatoes. Honey, white fungus, lily bulb — these show up in soups and teas. A little warming spice can help too, like fresh ginger or cinnamon. Use them in moderation. And if you tend to run hot, go easy on the ginger. Root vegetables are also great because they’re grounding. And as the weather cools, ease up on cold and raw foods. They can be harder on digestion this time of year. Think warm, cooked, soothing meals. It doesn’t have to be complicated. One warm soup a week counts.

And what if you notice yourself feeling a bit more sad or melancholic? That’s very normal. It’s the Metal element’s emotional current. The invitation is not to fix it, but to allow space for those feelings to rise and move through you. Let yourself process. That’s what the season is for. If it stays heavy for a long time, that’s a different matter. But a gentle autumn melancholy is not a problem to solve. It’s a note in the music of the year.

You might also wonder about the early rising advice. I’m not a morning person either, so let me translate. The rooster thing was ancient shorthand for living with the sun. In autumn, the sun rises later than in summer. So this is actually permission to sleep in a little longer. The core principle is to synchronize your rhythm with the season. That means honoring the increase of darkness — the Yin — and the decrease of light — the Yang. Don’t force a rigid schedule. Listen to your body and the natural light outside. If you wake with the dawn, that’s good. If you’re still in bed when the sun is up, that’s okay too. The point is to respect rest.

Now, you can see why so many of us feel burned out in the fall. Our culture runs on a model of never-ending growth. Every quarter has to be better than the last. The back-to-school push, the end-of-year targets — these are spring-like energies. Spring is about bursting forth, new growth. When we force that onto autumn, we create an internal clash. Nature is contracting, and we’re trying to expand. The world is quieting down, and we’re speeding up. It’s like swimming against a current. You might move forward for a while, but eventually, you’re exhausted.

I know that feeling personally. For years, I dreaded autumn. The darkening days made me restless. So I’d cope by doubling down, working late, filling every minute. One October afternoon, I was grinding through a project, and I felt hollow inside. Empty. I glanced outside. The maple trees were blazing with color, dropping leaves with such ease. And I thought, those maples are healthier than I am. They weren’t fighting. They were moving with the season. That moment cracked something open for me. I realized I needed to stop struggling and start flowing.

The trees are exhaling. The humans are inhaling. Something about that picture is deeply out of sync.

From the Neijing’s perspective, a lot of the anxiety and fatigue we feel in autumn comes from this mismatch. We ignore the wisdom of our own bodies and the environment we’re part of. Embracing Rong Ping — that calm, settling energy — is a quiet permission, written two thousand years ago, to stop pushing and start gathering. True strength for the winter ahead comes not from more activity, but from thoughtful consolidation. Letting go isn’t defeat. It’s an act of grace.

So here’s what I invite you to do this week. Find one small thing you can release. An old magazine. A lingering grudge. A task on your to-do list that’s been there forever and doesn’t matter. Let it go. Notice the space it creates. That space is the beginning of living in harmony with autumn. The trees have been doing this for millions of years. They know what they’re doing. And with a little practice, so can we.

And when the autumn marketing buzz cranks up again — buy the planner, sharpen the pencils, get back to business — close the notebook. Step outside. Watch the leaves drift. That’s enough.

So to wrap up: autumn in the Neijing is called Rong Ping — calm containment. It’s a season for gathering, not expanding. The Lungs and the Metal element guide us to process grief, to let go of what’s finished. Small daily shifts — dim evenings, harvest walks, letting-go rituals, mindful breathing — can align us with this energy. And when we stop fighting the season, we find something gentle and true. The great exhale. And that’s the wisdom autumn offers us. Not through hustle, but through grace.
