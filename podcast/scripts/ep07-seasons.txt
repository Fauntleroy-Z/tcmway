I was sleeping enough. I was eating well. Nothing had changed in my life.

I tried the usual fixes. I drank more water. I went for evening walks. I told myself to calm down, to breathe, to count to ten. None of it worked. Because here's the thing: the problem wasn't in my head. It wasn't in my schedule. It wasn't a lack of self-control. The problem was in my body. In a place and a system I'd never been taught to think about.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, the human body doesn't follow the Gregorian calendar. It follows something much older. An internal clock that's been running for thousands and thousands of years, synced to the sun, the soil, the sky, the length of the day, the angle of the light. And here's the first thing that surprised me: in TCM, the year isn't divided into four seasons. It's divided into five.

Spring. Summer. Late Summer. Autumn. Winter.

I know what you're thinking. Late Summer? What in the world is Late Summer?

Late Summer is this hidden, secret fifth season. And it's the key to understanding why you sometimes feel completely out of sync with the world around you. It's a transitional period. About eighteen days at the end of summer, right before autumn really kicks in. The air is thick and humid. The ground is warm. Everything is ripening, heavy with fruit. It smells like earth and growth.

And every one of these five seasons is paired with an element, and a pair of organ systems. This is not vague poetry. This is a clinical observation that's been tested and refined over more than two thousand years.

Let me walk you through the map. Because once you see it, you can't unsee it.

Spring belongs to the Wood element. Think of new growth pushing up through soil — green, determined, full of life. This season governs your Liver and Gallbladder. It's the energy of planning, of vision, of starting things.

Summer belongs to Fire. Heat, expansion, brightness, the sun at its peak. It governs your Heart and Small Intestine. It's the energy of joy, connection, outward expression.

Late Summer belongs to Earth. Stability, nourishment, the ground beneath your feet. It governs your Spleen and Stomach — the bedrock of your entire digestive system. This is the energy of groundedness, of being at home in your own body.

Autumn belongs to Metal. Sharpness, clarity, precision, the crispness of the first cold morning. It governs your Lungs and Large Intestine. It's the energy of discernment and letting go.

Winter belongs to Water. Depth, stillness, storage, the quiet darkness under ice. It governs your Kidneys and Bladder. It's the energy of rest, conservation, deep reserves.

Now stay with me, because this next part is the thing that completely rearranged how I see myself.

When you feel "off" during a certain time of year — anxious and restless in summer, heavy and weepy in autumn, completely drained and exhausted in winter — that's not a character flaw. It's not a mood disorder. It's not something wrong with your personality. It's your body responding to the dominant energy of the season. Your organ systems are shifting. They're recalibrating. And they're sending you signals.

The question is — are you listening? Or are you treating every sign of imbalance as something to ignore, push through, medicate away?

I want to tell you what happened to me. Because it happened in summer. In July. Right around now.

In TCM, the season of summer is ruled by the Fire element. And the organ that belongs to Fire is the Heart. Now here's where this gets interesting — and this is a concept I want you to hold onto.

The Heart in Chinese medicine is much, much more than a pump. It's considered the emperor. The ruler. The organ that all the other organs take direction from. It houses something called the Shen. S-H-E-N. The Shen is roughly what we would call the mind, the consciousness, the spirit. It's the seat of our emotional life. Our joy. Our ability to connect with other people. Our charisma. Our warmth.

When the Heart energy — the Heart Qi [chee] — is balanced, you feel joyful. You feel calm. You sleep deeply through the night. You wake up rested. There's a warmth about you that other people notice. You're present. You're grounded. You have a kind of radiance.

But when summer's heat becomes too much — and I'm not just talking about the temperature outside — that excess Fire can agitate the Heart. The emperor gets overheated. And when the emperor is uncomfortable, the whole kingdom suffers.

TCM has a name for this state. Heart Fire.

Let me describe what Heart Fire feels like. See if any of this rings a bell.

That irritability. The short fuse. Someone says something minor and you snap. You feel bad about it afterward, but in the moment, it just comes out.

Insomnia. Or that thing where you're technically asleep but your dreams are so vivid and restless that you wake up feeling like you never slept at all.

Anxiety. That fluttery, unsettled feeling in your chest. Heart palpitations. Like your heart is knocking on your ribs, asking to be let out.

A flushed face. Your cheeks feel hot. You look in the mirror and you're redder than usual. Like you've been running.

Mouth ulcers. Canker sores. A tongue that looks more red than pink, maybe with a yellow coating.

In TCM, there's a saying: the Heart channel opens to the tongue. It means the state of your Heart energy directly affects your speech. Your words. How you talk to people. When excess Fire rises up inside you, it comes out. Through your mouth. As sharp, cutting, impatient words.

Suddenly my wife's comment made complete sense. I wasn't choosing to be difficult. I wasn't trying to be mean. My Heart Fire was literally flaring up through my voice. So what do you actually do about it? How do you cool down when the fire isn't just outside, but inside?

For me, the answer turned out to be surprisingly simple. It came from a flower. A small, white, yellow-centered flower.

Chrysanthemum.

Chrysanthemum flowers — Ju Hua, J-U H-U-A, in Chinese — have been used for centuries as a gentle cooling herb. They clear heat from the upper body. From the head. From the eyes. From the Heart. They're gentle. They don't knock you out. They don't sedate you. They just... cool things down. Quietly. Steadily.

I started making chrysanthemum tea in the afternoons. A small handful of dried flowers in a cup. Hot water — not boiling, just hot. Let it steep for a few minutes. The taste is light, slightly sweet, vaguely floral. It's pleasant. It doesn't feel medicinal. It feels like taking a deep breath.

Now I want to stop here and give you an honest note. Chrysanthemum is slightly cold in its nature. That's how it works — by introducing a gentle cooling energy into your system. But if you're someone who tends to have loose stools, or you feel cold easily, or your digestion is frequently upset — those can be signs that your Spleen energy, your digestive fire, is already on the weaker side.

In that case, use chrysanthemum carefully. Not every day. Or add a slice of fresh ginger to the tea. The ginger warms your core while the chrysanthemum cools your head. The two herbs balance each other out. That's one of the beautiful things about Chinese herbal thinking. It's never just one thing against one symptom. It's always about balance.

About a week after I started this chrysanthemum tea habit, my wife — the same person who told me I was being a jerk — she looked at me across the dinner table, stopped for a long moment, and said: "You're back."

I asked her what she meant. She said, "You're not snapping. You're not irritated. You're... you again."

Let me quickly walk you through the other seasons. Because once you have this mental map, you'll start noticing things you never noticed before.

Spring is the Wood season. It's the time of new growth, new beginnings. Your Liver energy — that's your body's internal planner, your strategist — is most active right now. This is the season to rise early, to take brisk walks outside, to move your body after the winter stillness. Eat green things. Leafy greens, sprouts, a squeeze of lemon. Sour flavors gently support the Liver. Your body wants to move. Let it move.

Summer — we've covered this. Fire. The Heart. Stay cool. Rest at midday if you can — even a twenty-minute lie-down after lunch makes a real difference. Eat cucumber, watermelon, mung beans, bitter greens. Foods that genuinely beat the heat from the inside out. And maybe try chrysanthemum tea.

Late Summer is the Earth season. The transition. Those thick, humid days when you feel heavy and sluggish and nothing seems to move quickly. This is when your digestive system — your Spleen and Stomach — needs more support than usual. Eat cooked, warm foods. Naturally sweet, yellow and orange things. Squash. Carrots. Sweet potatoes. Millet. Not raw salads and ice-cold smoothies — your digestion is already working overtime during seasonal transitions. Be kind to it.

Autumn belongs to Metal. The air gets dry. There's a crispness, a sharpness. This is Lung season. Your respiratory system is at center stage. You need moistening, hydrating foods. Pears. Radishes. Onions. Almonds. Pungent flavors that open the chest and help you breathe more freely. Go to bed a little earlier than you did in summer. The natural world is drawing inward. So should you.

And Winter. The Water season. The time of the Kidneys. This is the season of storage. Conservation. Deep, genuine rest. Go to bed early. Rise late — let the sun wake you if you can. Sleep more. That's not laziness. That's biology. Your ancestors did it. Your body is designed for it. Eat warm, nourishing, deeply grounding foods. Bone broths. Root vegetables. Seaweed. Beans. Black-colored foods like black sesame and black beans, which in Chinese medicine are traditionally associated with Kidney health. Foods that feel like a hug from the inside.

The simplest, most elegant rule of all? Eat what's growing near you, right now, in this season. Your great-grandparents didn't analyze macronutrients. They ate what the earth gave them. A crisp cucumber in July. A dense, sweet potato in January. Your body evolved expecting that exact rhythm. This is not a diet. It's not a protocol. It's the ancestral blueprint.

Now let me warn you about something. The danger zones. The times when your body is most vulnerable.

Have you ever noticed that you get sick right when the seasons change? You're fine all summer, then September arrives, and suddenly you're coughing. That's not bad luck. That's not a random virus. Remember those eighteen-day transitional windows I mentioned? They happen at the end of every season. Not just summer. Spring. Autumn. Winter. Each one has this eighteen-day pivot point. I want to leave you with a contrast that I come back to again and again.

The modern Western instinct is to fight the season. When it's hot, we blast the air conditioning, chug ice water, eat ice cream. We treat the weather like something to defeat. When it's cold, we seal ourselves indoors and crank the heat like we're trying to pretend winter doesn't exist.

The Traditional Chinese Medicine approach is fundamentally different. It's not about fighting. It's not about winning. It's about adapting. Harmonizing. Working with the energy rather than against it.

Here's the classic example that blows people's minds. Drinking warm liquids in summer. I know. It sounds wrong. Your whole life, you've been told to drink cold things when you're hot. The colder the better. Ice in everything.

A feeling of heaviness. Fatigue that you can't explain. A kind of internal dampness that makes everything feel like you're walking through mud.

A cup of warm mint tea on a hot day? That's energetically cooling without shocking your system. The mint cools. The warmth doesn't extinguish your digestive fire. It works with your body instead of against it.
