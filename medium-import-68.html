Appetite Is Your Body's Health Barometer

COMICMARKER68

Black coffee at seven in the morning quiets an empty stomach for a little while. You answer emails, push through midday meetings, and skip lunch without thinking twice about the hollow ache beneath your ribs.

By three in the afternoon, the quiet breaks. Your hands tremble above the keyboard, and you head straight for the pantry in search of quick crackers or sugar.

Outside on a low branch, Ollie the screech owl perches with his ear tufts flat and one foot tucked into his feathers. He catches the softest rustle long before a branch snaps.

In Chinese medicine, appetite works like an internal gauge. Spleen and Stomach here name a working partnership, not the two organs a surgeon would lift out and weigh. The Stomach takes food in and ripens it. Its Qi descends. The Spleen transforms what the Stomach has ripened, moves it onward, and raises the clear part as usable Qi and Blood. When balanced, hunger arrives near meal hours, eating feels restorative, and energy holds.

This relationship sits at the center of classical practice. In 1249, during the Mongol period, physician Li Dongyuan wrote the Pi Wei Lun. His claim was blunt: damage the Spleen and Stomach, and illness can surface almost anywhere. In the Qing dynasty, Ye Tianshi sharpened the split. The Spleen fears Dampness; the Stomach runs on fluids and fears dryness.

In the old Zi Wu Liu Zhu clock, the Stomach gets seven to nine in the morning and the Spleen takes nine to eleven. Coffee on an empty stomach lifts you without giving the Spleen anything to work with. The traditional pattern is food first, coffee after: something warm and cooked before the cold shock of caffeine.

Low appetite with lingering fatigue usually points to Spleen Qi deficiency, especially when the tongue is pale, the stools are loose, and meals leave you heavy. Add cold hands, a craving for hot cooked food, and discomfort after anything iced, and the reading shifts to Spleen Yang deficiency—the same weak Qi, minus its warmth. Turning food into Qi takes heat. A cold middle has none to spare; a Hot pattern reacts differently.

When Dampness is the problem, the limbs feel sandbagged and the head feels wrapped in damp wool. The tongue carries a thick white coating. That three o'clock crash belongs here too, if breakfast and lunch never arrived. Caffeine and stress can produce the same scene. The tremor is a clue, not a diagnosis.

Eat late and heavy, and the Stomach stops moving things down. A sour belch at breakfast, a thick greasy coating, and lingering fullness suggest Food Stagnation. A wiry pulse points to Liver Qi pressing on the Stomach's descent. Red or slightly swollen tongue sides matter only when the stress and rib-side tightness are there too. Feeling full early could be a dozen things.

Hunger can also become excessive. Stomach Fire is the opposite problem. Hunger comes on hard, returns soon after eating, and arrives with bad breath, tender gums, and a red tongue under a thick yellow coat. Strong hunger with thirst and unintended weight loss can point to diabetes, whose classical description as Xiao Ke overlaps with this Fire picture.

Stomach Yin deficiency brings a different hunger: a gnawing emptiness with little desire to eat. Dry mouth and throat favor small sips; dry stool or dry retching may appear. The tongue looks red with a bald spot at the center, and a practitioner would find a thin, quick pulse.

Dried ginger (Gan Jiang) warms a cold, damp middle. Fresh ginger (Sheng Jiang) is the one for nausea and chills. Neither suits a dry, overheated Stomach. For a weak Spleen, congee, squash, and soft-cooked egg are easy work. A dry Stomach does better with pear, millet porridge, and room-temperature sips.

Then there is the hunger stress erases. Sudden fright throws Qi into disorder, drying the mouth or turning food mildly nauseating. Sustained worry knots it instead: Su Wen 39 describes pensiveness binding Qi, while Su Wen 5 associates pensiveness with injury to the Spleen.

Some signs sit outside the congee pot: appetite that never comes back, weight dropping on its own, trouble swallowing, repeated vomiting, or fullness that will not clear belong to clinical assessment.

Vomiting blood, black tarry stools, severe abdominal pain, severe dehydration, or confusion are emergencies. Shaking, sweating, or confusion can also be a low-blood-glucose safety concern for people taking glucose-lowering medication.

For one week, notice four things.

Hunger: steady signal, anxious tremor, or absent?

First bites: pleasant, or heavy at once?

After eating: clear mind, or fog and bloating?

Three in the afternoon: steady, shaky, or craving sugar?

At your next meal, notice the first five bites without a screen. Do they taste good, or go down like a chore? In the old pattern, appetite that has vanished calls for a small warm breakfast, not a full plate.

The old pattern is warm food before coffee. Watching three o'clock is the experiment.
