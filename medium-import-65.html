Your Insomnia Has a Type — A TCM Field Guide

COMICMARKER65

The clock reads 2:14 a.m. My heart is a snare drum, and a grudge from 2019 is back in the room. I fell asleep fine. That is the insult.

I lie there rerunning it, jaw tight, shoulders near my ears. This is not random: my body woke me on purpose — a particular hour, a particular feeling, the same pairing for weeks.

Insomnia is one word for a dozen different nights. Traditional Chinese medicine reads the hour you wake — and the way you wake — as a message with a type.

Six portraits follow, plus a seventh that needs a practitioner's hand. Each night points to a zheng, a pattern: how you look, when you wake, what you ate. Three come from the clock; the rest from how sleep felt.

Type One: The Mind That Won't Switch Off

The classic evening vigil: exhausted on the sofa, then forty-two tabs the moment your head hits the pillow. This belongs to the Heart and the shen — often to the Liver, which stores the Blood. Suwen 10: "when a person lies down, the Blood returns to the Liver." When Liver Blood runs thin, the hun has no mattress, and the shen turns petty, recalculating your mortgage. Suan Zao Ren Tang, traditionally attributed to Zhang Zhongjing's Jin Gui Yao Lue, speaks to that Blood-deficient picture — a practitioner's prescription, not something to order online. Traditional dietary approaches here emphasize warm, early meals: dark greens, red dates.

Type Two: The 1 to 3 a.m. Alarm

You fall asleep fine, then snap awake between 1 and 3, alert, warm, annoyed, jaw clenched. The Liver governs smooth movement of Qi; 1 to 3 a.m. is its window on the organ clock. Three tongues, three nights: red with a yellow coat means stagnation has turned to heat; thin and white means it hasn't; red and nearly bare means there was never anything to move — that's empty heat from thin Liver Yin and Blood. Your pulse is the one sign you can't take yourself — a practitioner's fingers feel for the wiry, taut line — so note the hour and tongue, and bring both to the table.

Type Three: The 3 to 5 a.m. Club

Between 3 and 5 a.m. the waking is cool and strange: shallow breath, a constricted chest, a house too cold, too quiet. This hour belongs to the Lungs and the po, the corporeal soul. Grief disperses Qi — the Suwen's 悲则气消 — and dispersed Qi doesn't descend. Nothing heroic helps. So the practice stayed small: a letter, written and never sent.

Type Four: The Watchman Who Startles

A creaking floorboard, a screen rattling in the wind, and the heart leaps. Vigilance disguised as rest: dreams at full volume, waking as if you'd spent eight hours on perimeter duty around an empty warehouse. Heart and Gallbladder Qi deficiency. Unlike Type One's tabs, this wakefulness is reactive — sounds, not rumination. The old answer: reserves over vigilance — warm meals, early nights, less caffeine after noon.

Type Five: Slept Eight Hours, Woke Wrecked

Lights out at 10:30, barely a shift, awake at 7, wrecked anyway. You slept; the sleep did nothing. Head full of wet cotton, limbs leaden, tongue pale and greasy. Spleen and dampness: a digestive core too sluggish to transform a heavy, late, or dairy-rich dinner.

Type Six: The Kitchen Still Running at Bedtime

The Suwen's seven characters: wei bu he ze wo bu an — when the stomach is not at peace, sleep is not either. Digestion needs Stomach Qi to descend; sleep needs defensive yang to withdraw inward. In the tradition's reading, a stomach still churning a 9:15 dinner blocks that retreat. No body locks its doors while the kitchen is still running. Dampness leaves you heavy but asleep; stomach discord keeps you awake.

Type Seven: The Night That Gives Nothing Back

Phlegm-heat disturbing the Heart. You slept seven hours, maybe eight, and woke with your chest heavy, your mouth tasting of bitter grease. The tongue tells: red, thick with a yellow coat; the pulse slippery and fast. Wen Dan Tang — Chen Yan's San Yin Ji Yi Bing Zheng Fang Lun, 1174 — is the classical answer. The formulas named here are practitioner-adjusted prescriptions, not shelf products — get the pattern backwards and the nights get worse. Traditional dietary discussions emphasize lighter evening meals and less late alcohol.

Where the Map Ends

Classical theory reads every pattern above as one failed handover: when Wei Qi (the defensive yang) won't enter the Yin, the eyes stay open. Two windows stay blank on purpose: 11 p.m. to 1 a.m. is the Gallbladder's own; 5 to 7 a.m. is a body facing the day. Patterns stack: a 1 a.m. Liver wake on a foggy morning is one night, not two — bring the whole list. Zhang Jingyue split these nights in two — something lodged, or nothing left to hold it. This is vocabulary, not a diagnosis. So the routine stays small: lights down by nine, tomorrow's list on paper before bed. If the same hour keeps waking you for a month, take it to a practitioner. Severe or months-long sleep disruption deserves a doctor; modern guidelines make CBT-I, not a pill, first-line care, and apnea, restless legs, thyroid disease, and depression all impersonate these patterns.

That 2:14 wake-up was Type Two; the jaw and the grudge were the tell. So the log stays on the nightstand: the hour, the jaw, the tongue in the mirror — three weeks of that beats any story.
