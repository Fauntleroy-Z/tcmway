The Season of Letting Go: What the Neijing Knew About Autumn 2,000 Years Ago

Every September, I feel a strange pull. The air gets crisp, the light slants a little lower, and the world seems to shout, “Back to school! Back to the hustle! New season, new goals!” But my body whispers something entirely different. It says: slow down, go inward, rest.

For years, I thought something was wrong with me. Why did I feel this melancholy urge to retreat when everyone else was ramping up? I’d fight it, scheduling new projects and filling my calendar, only to feel drained and brittle by November. Then I read the Huangdi Neijing (The Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon), and a 2,000-year-old passage gave me the permission I didn’t know I needed.

In its second chapter, a guide to living in harmony with the four seasons, the Neijing lays out a radical vision for autumn. It’s a vision that runs completely counter to our modern productivity culture. It’s a season not for starting, but for finishing. Not for expanding, but for consolidating. It’s the great, necessary season of letting go.

Ollie, my screech owl companion who lives by these rhythms without ever needing to be told, finds our species baffling. Every creature on earth knows autumn means one thing: prepare. You humans? You see falling leaves and think "time for a new side hustle." He is, unfortunately, not wrong.

Let's look at what the text says directly. This is the foundation for understanding the entire season.

秋三月，此谓容平。天气以急，地气以明。早卧早起，与鸡俱兴。使志安宁，以缓秋刑。收敛神气，使秋气平。无外其志，使肺气清。此秋气之应，养收之道也。逆之则伤肺，冬为飧泄，奉藏者少。

The three months of autumn are called Rong Ping — containment and tranquility. The Qi of heaven becomes urgent; the Qi of earth becomes clear. Go to bed early and rise early, with the rooster. Keep your mind peaceful and calm, to mitigate the punishing aspect of autumn. Gather and restrain the spirit, to keep the autumn Qi balanced. Do not extend your will outward, to keep the lung Qi clear. This is the way of nourishing the harvest. Going against this harms the lungs, and in winter one will suffer from undigested diarrhea, with insufficient resources to support storage.

The Harvest Principle: Why Autumn Asks You to Stop

The key to the entire passage is the first phrase: 容平 (Rong Ping). Let's break that down.

Rong (容) means to contain, to hold, to gather in. Think of a farmer at the end of the season. They aren’t planting new seeds; they are harvesting what has grown and storing it in the granary for the winter. This is the energy of Rong. It’s about bringing your energy back from the external world and consolidating your resources.

Ping (平) means level, calm, balanced, and peaceful. After the vibrant, chaotic, and expansive energy of summer, autumn brings a quiet stillness. The Qi of the world is no longer bursting forth; it’s settling down. The landscape itself becomes serene and clear as things begin to decay and return to the earth.

So, Rong Ping means “containment and tranquility.” It’s a state of active, intentional gathering and calming. It’s not laziness or depression. A tree dropping its leaves isn’t failing; it’s demonstrating profound wisdom. It’s letting go of what it can’t sustain through the winter to protect its core vitality. That, in a nutshell, is the spirit of autumn.

Our culture gets this backward. We treat September like a second January, a time for new resolutions and frantic activity. The Neijing suggests this is a profound mistake. Pushing to expand when nature is contracting creates a deep imbalance, one that leaves us feeling scattered, dry, and depleted.

The Physics of Sadness: Metal, Lungs, and Letting Go

In the framework of the Five Elements, autumn belongs to Metal. The organs associated with the Metal element are the Lungs (肺, Fei) and their Yang partner, the Large Intestine (大肠, Da Chang). This connection is the key to understanding autumn’s physical and emotional landscape.

The Lungs are considered the most delicate, or "tender," organ. They are the bridge between our inner world and the outer environment, taking in the air and governing the body’s first line of defense—the skin and body hair (肺主皮毛, Fei zhu pi mao). This is why, as the autumn air turns dry, our skin, lips, and throat often follow suit. The Lungs are easily affected by dryness, and the season’s climate reflects that vulnerability.

The emotion associated with the Lungs and the Metal element is grief or sadness (悲, Bei). This is a crucial point. That feeling of melancholy that creeps in as the leaves turn gold isn’t a personal failing; it’s a seasonal resonance. The energy of autumn is about decline, return, and loss. The vibrant green of summer is gone. The long, warm days are gone. Nature is modeling a beautiful, necessary process of release, and our bodies and spirits feel it.

The Neijing advises us to “keep your mind peaceful and calm, to mitigate the punishing aspect of autumn” (使志安宁，以缓秋刑, shi zhi an ning, yi huan qiu xing). The "punishing" or "sobering" aspect of autumn is this very quality of decline and loss. By acknowledging and accepting the season’s inherent sadness, we soften its edge. We allow ourselves to feel it, process it, and let it go.

This is where the Large Intestine comes in. Its function is elegantly simple: to receive waste from the Small Intestine and release what is no longer needed. It is the body’s organ of letting go. Emotionally and spiritually, autumn is the time to do the same. It’s the season to take stock of our lives, our projects, and our emotional attachments, and ask: What is no longer serving me? What can I release to conserve my energy for the deep rest of winter?

Ollie, perched on the arm of my reading chair with his ear tufts flattened in contemplation, clicked his beak thoughtfully, and offered this: You humans are the only creatures who feel guilty about feeling sad when the whole world is grieving. Even we owls get a bit melancholy watching the leaves fall. The difference is, we do not resist it.

I learned this the hard way a few years ago. I took on a massive new project in October, working late nights and pushing through the fatigue. I ignored the dry cough that started and the feeling of brittleness in my soul. By December, I was completely burned out. I caught a nasty cold that morphed into bronchitis and lingered until February. My body was simply enacting the Neijing's warning: I had failed to "nourish the harvest," and I entered winter with an empty storehouse.

Actionable Tips: Living an Autumn of Rong Ping

Understanding the theory is one thing; living it is another. So how do we apply these 2,000-year-old principles in our modern lives?

1. Adjust Your Sleep Schedule: “Go to bed early and rise early, with the rooster.”
This instruction is about aligning with the sun. As the days get shorter, our sleep patterns should shift. In autumn, this means going to bed earlier than you did in the summer, ideally by 10 or 11 p.m., and waking as the sun rises, around 6 or 7 a.m. This syncs your body’s rhythm with the natural cycle of light and dark, helping to conserve your Qi and Yang energy.

2. Eat for the Lungs: Moisten and Gather.
Since autumn is dry and the Lungs dislike dryness, the diet should focus on moistening foods. Pears are the quintessential autumn fruit for this reason. Other excellent choices include Yin Er (white fungus), Bai He (lily bulb), and Lian Zi (lotus seed). Warm, cooked foods are best. Soups and stews are perfect for this time of year.

The Neijing also advises us to "gather and restrain the spirit." In dietary terms, this corresponds to the sour flavor, which has an astringent (收敛, shou lian) or "gathering in" quality. Adding small amounts of sour foods like lemon, sourdough, or sauerkraut can help pull your energy inward. At the same time, it’s wise to reduce pungent and spicy foods, which are dispersing and can further dry out the Lungs.

3. Breathe with Intention.
The Lungs are the only organ we can consciously control to such a degree. Deep, slow, diaphragmatic breathing is one of the most powerful ways to support Lung Qi. Take five minutes each morning or evening to sit quietly and focus on your breath. Inhale deeply into your belly, hold for a moment, and exhale slowly and completely. This simple act calms the nervous system and directly tonifies the organ of the season.

4. Let Go.
This is the spiritual work of autumn. Look at your commitments. Are there projects you can wrap up and complete, rather than starting new ones? Are there old resentments or griefs you’ve been carrying? Autumn is the time to journal, reflect, and consciously release them. Clean out a closet. Donate old clothes. Create physical and emotional space. This mirrors the Large Intestine's function and clears the way for winter's quiet introspection.

The Winter Warning: What Happens When We Ignore Autumn

The Neijing is very clear about the consequences of fighting the season: “Going against this harms the lungs, and in winter one will suffer from undigested diarrhea, with insufficient resources to support storage.”

Let’s unpack that. "Harming the lungs" means weakening your immune system, making you more susceptible to colds, flu, and coughs. It also manifests as the dryness we discussed—dry skin, a dry throat, and a brittle feeling.

The phrase "winter will bring undigested diarrhea" (冬为飧泄, dong wei sun xie) is a powerful metaphor. On a literal level, it points to a breakdown in the digestive system. The energy you were supposed to be "harvesting" in autumn is needed to fuel your digestive fire (your Spleen and Stomach Yang) through the winter. If you arrive in winter already depleted, your body won't have the resources to properly transform food, leading to issues like loose stools with undigested food particles.

Metaphorically, it means you enter the most taxing part of the year with no reserves. You’re running on fumes. You become prone to burnout, exhaustion, and that deep, bone-weary feeling that can settle in around the holidays. By respecting autumn's call to slow down, you are making a direct deposit into your health savings account for the winter ahead.

Autumn isn't about stopping your life. It's about shifting your focus from outward expansion to inward consolidation. It’s a season of profound wisdom, teaching us the beautiful and necessary rhythm of release. By listening to its message, we do more than just get through the season; we align ourselves with a powerful, natural current that nourishes our bodies and calms our spirits.

This year, try a small experiment. When September rolls around and the world screams "hustle," listen for the quieter whisper from your own body. Give yourself permission to say "no" to one new thing and "yes" to an evening of rest. You might just find that in letting go, you gain exactly what you need.

FAQ

Is autumn sadness the same as seasonal affective disorder (SAD)?

This is a great question. While they might feel similar, the TCM perspective differs from the clinical diagnosis of Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD). The Neijing frames the melancholy of autumn as a natural, functional, and temporary emotional state that resonates with the season's energy of decline and letting go. It's not seen as a pathology to be eliminated, but as a feeling to be acknowledged and processed. SAD, on the other hand, is a clinical form of depression with specific diagnostic criteria that significantly impairs daily functioning. If you're experiencing severe symptoms, it's always best to consult with a qualified healthcare professional.

What foods support the Lungs in autumn according to TCM?

The focus in autumn is on nourishing Yin and moistening dryness. The star players are foods that are white in color (the color of the Metal element) and have a moistening quality. Pears, especially when gently cooked, are a classic choice. Others include Asian pears, apples, almonds, and seeds like sesame. Specific herbs often used in food therapy are Bai He (lily bulb), Yin Er (white fungus or tremella mushroom), and Lian Zi (lotus seed), which are wonderful in soups or sweet porridges. Adding a little bit of honey can also soothe a dry throat. It’s generally best to favor warm, cooked meals and reduce cold, raw foods that can be hard on the digestive system.

If I'm not a morning person, does "rise with the rooster" still apply?

The spirit of the Neijing's advice is about aligning with the natural rhythms of Yin and Yang, not about literally setting your alarm to a rooster's crow. The core principle is to follow the sun. In autumn, the sun rises later and sets earlier than in summer. So, the practical application is to shift your sleep schedule accordingly. If you were waking up at 7:30 a.m. in the summer, maybe you try for 7:00 a.m. in the autumn. The goal is to avoid the summer habit of staying up very late and sleeping in, which depletes your Yang Qi when you need to be conserving it for winter. It's about a gentle shift, not a rigid rule.
