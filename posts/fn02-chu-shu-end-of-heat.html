Chu Shu (End of Heat): The Day Summer Officially Lets Go

Ollie's Take

I stood in my kitchen last week, sweating over a pot of soup, and caught myself thinking the same thing I think every year around this time: this heat is supposed to be done by now. It wasn't. The calendar said late August, my phone said 33°C, and my shirt was stuck to my back just walking to the mailbox.

That's when I remembered it was almost Chu Shu [choo shoo], the End of Heat, one of the twenty-four solar terms in the old Chinese calendar. And here's the thing I got wrong for years — Chu Shu doesn't mean summer is over. It means summer's grip is starting to loosen. The heat doesn't vanish on this day, it just stops tightening its hold and begins, slowly, to let go.

There's actually a name for the heat that lingers past this point: qiu lao hu [chyoh laoww hoo], the "autumn tiger." I love this phrase because it's so honest about how transitions actually work. The tiger isn't the full blast of July anymore, it's sneakier, showing up in the afternoons after the mornings have already turned cool and tricked you into thinking you were safe. Anyone who's pulled out a sweater in September only to be sweating by noon knows exactly what this tiger feels like.

In parts of China, this is traditionally when families start eating duck. I asked a friend from Nanjing about this once and she laughed and said her grandmother would just say "chu shu chi ya," eat duck at Chu Shu, like it explained itself. Duck is considered cooling in the old food logic, a way of easing the body out of summer's heaviness without shocking it. It's less about fighting the heat now and more about meeting the season halfway, the same way you might start leaving a jacket by the door even though you don't need it yet.

Old almanacs used to break Chu Shu down into three phases, a kind of week-by-week forecast for how the season would unwind: first the eagles begin their hunting season, then heaven and earth take on a sterner, drier feel, and finally the grain ripens in the fields. I thought about that division the other evening, walking around the block after dinner while the light was still doing its long gold thing. The air had actually softened, that first real hint that the season was turning, and for a second I let myself believe it had fully arrived. Then the next afternoon I was back in the kitchen with the fan going, chopping vegetables in the kind of heat that has no business showing up after a morning like that. Mornings cool enough for a light jacket, afternoons still demanding the fan on high — that's the autumn tiger doing exactly what it does, showing up right when you've let your guard down.

That's really the shift I notice most this time of year, not in temperature but in attitude. All summer you're on guard, dodging the worst of the sun, planning around it. Chu Shu marks the point where that vigilance softens into something more like patience. You stop bracing against the heat and start just waiting it out, trusting that the tiger's visits will get shorter and further apart until one day they stop coming at all.

Ollie's aside: Even I noticed it this week — the cicadas outside my window went from that nonstop buzzing wall of sound to something patchier, almost hesitant, like they're losing interest in the argument. Give it a few more evenings and I bet the crickets take over the night shift entirely.

FAQ

So is autumn here after Chu Shu?

Not quite — Chu Shu marks the turning point, not the finish line. Think of it as summer easing off the gas rather than autumn arriving all at once.

Why does everyone mention duck?

It's a long-standing food tradition in parts of China, tied to the idea that duck is "cooling" and helps the body transition gently out of summer. It's cultural custom rather than any kind of medical guidance.
