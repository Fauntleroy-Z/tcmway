What Classical Chinese Medicine Means by “Escorting Out” an Illness

COMICMARKER66

A Cold Morning on the Platform

I stood on a damp October platform, collar up against a wind with teeth, watching the departure board push the 7:40 back another four minutes.

By Tuesday my throat was scraped raw and my shoulders locked under the office vents. I counterattacked: zinc, nasal spray, three teas in one mug.

By Friday I sounded like a rusted hinge.

Nothing I did had direction. The phrase I keep coming back to is 给邪以出路 — give the Xie Qi, the pathogenic influence, a way out. Out through the surface, down through the bowel, or back up and out. "Immune support" is our translation, not theirs.

What the Old Texts Could Not See

Ancient physicians never saw a virus. Wind, cold, dampness, heat — not influenza in code. Wind meant something sudden and shifting that hit the surface first; bound with cold, it stiffened the neck and back.

Chinese medicine reads signs into patterns: chills, thirst, sweat, pulse, tongue. The Su Wen, chapter 33, says it in eight characters — 邪之所凑，其气必虚 — where the pathogenic influence gathers, the Qi there was already thin. Read it locally, and you get why a cold goes for my neck and my coworker's gut.

Patterns Instead of a Timetable

Six channels get taught as a staircase. The Shang Han Lun, credited to Zhang Zhongjing around 200 CE, never worked that neatly. Patterns overlapped, and some illnesses landed directly in the Yin divisions.

Taiyang is the surface picture: chills, stiff neck, floating pulse. Yangming runs hot in two different ways: drenching interior heat, or heat that has dried the bowel until nothing moves. With Shaoyang, chills and heat trade places, and a bitter taste lingers that water will not rinse away.

Taiyang wind-strike means sweating with chills and a floating slack pulse, classically Wei Qi and Ying Qi out of step; cold-damage means no sweat, body aches, a floating tight pulse. The classics pair them with Gui Zhi Tang and Ma Huang Tang — prescriptions, not items to choose from a symptom list. Ma Huang is ephedra, able to raise heart rate and blood pressure and interact with medicines — both need qualified assessment.

Directions and Exits

The logic began with location. Surface trouble called for opening the surface. Excess stuck in the bowel meant moving things down, and something lodged high up had to come back up and out. Read the location wrong and treatment pushed the illness deeper instead of out. Sweat someone whose illness has already moved inward and the chills may stop, but you have spent fluids the body needed — and the classics are blunt about what follows: palpitations, dizziness, a patient weaker than before.

In the 1730s, Cheng Guopeng sorted all of it into eight methods. Three are exits with different addresses: out through the skin, up and out, down and out. A fourth moves nothing — it cools heat until it stops burning. The rest are warming, building back, breaking up what has stalled. One stops pushing and works the hinge — harmonizing, the Shaoyang answer. The upward exit is mostly retired; self-induced vomiting risks aspiration and electrolyte crashes, not a home remedy.

Watching the Shift

By Sunday night, day six of the same cold, I added a second duvet and sweated through it anyway. Ollie, the owl who has been editing my health decisions from the banister for years, flattened his ear tufts, rotated his head a few degrees too far, and let one eyelid drop. "I once sat on a radiator until my talons grew hot," he said. "Being an owl did not make me wise. More heat is not an open door."

By Monday morning I was under two blankets, nose running clear, wanting only hot water. By Wednesday I had kicked the blankets off, my throat was burning, and the only relief was cold water straight from the tap.

Three days apart, the same body told a different story. Chills and clear discharge suggest something still at the surface. A burning throat and thirst for cold water is heat that has turned inward and upward — the picture warm-disease doctors mapped two ways over, Ye Tianshi tracking depth through the four layers — Wei, Qi, Ying, Xue — Wu Jutong tracking it through the three burners, beginning at the Lung and descending. Either way it is early heat moving in, not the drenching-sweat Yangming heat, and not the slow Yin-deficiency heat that builds over months. Neither proves anything alone; tongue, pulse, and the whole course matter.

My raw throat came before the chills. Cold damage usually begins with chills; heat that shows up first belongs on the warm-disease map.

Most colds clear up in a week either way. What the tradition adds is direction — read the body's signs and stop working against them.

Aiming would have looked boring. Monday, chilled and wanting hot water, I reached for warmth and rest, not a draft to prove something. By Wednesday, throat burning and wanting the tap, I wanted cool fluids and the second duvet back in the cupboard. Same cold, opposite move, three days apart.

When to Stop Taking Notes and Call Someone

Some things skip the notebook. Trouble breathing, chest pain, new confusion, high fever with stiff neck, vomiting that will not stop, nothing staying down, or weakness that flattens you: any one belongs in same-day urgent assessment. Pregnancy, infancy, older age, and chronic illness lower the threshold.

Write down four things: sweating or dry, hot or cold, blanket up or kicked off, thirsty for hot or cold. None treats anything, but it is the difference between walking into a clinic with a headline and walking in with a picture.
