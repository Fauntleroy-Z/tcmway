Frost's Descent: The Last 15 Days of Autumn

COMICMARKER67

A blade on glass woke me, the dry rasp of someone scraping a windshield. The pipes clanked. My bare feet hit the cold floor, and I wanted broth before tea.

Out by the woodpile, Ollie, our resident screech owl, had fluffed down in the hollow of a tall oak. The puddle beside the logs had a thin lid of ice. He gave it one slow blink, tucked a foot into his breast feathers, and went still.

The Turn

Frost's Descent, or Shuangjiang, is the eighteenth solar term, arriving in late October as autumn's final fifteen days. Early on, the afternoon may still feel like dry September, with leftover summer heat. Later, frost can stay on the windshield past sunrise, and cold sits behind the dryness. Same cracked lips, different cause.

Metal and the Lung

In traditional Chinese medicine, autumn corresponds to Metal, paired with the Lung and Large Intestine. Its climate is dryness. The Suwen, first half of the Inner Classic, says it in four characters: when dryness prevails, things wither. That reach is systemic; a scratchy throat and hard, dry stool share one seasonal picture.

The Lung is delicate: it meets weather at the nose and, in the old account, through every pore. Its Qi governs that exchange. The Lung spreads Wei Qi to the surface, where it runs outside the vessels. Wei Qi comes from food and grain: the Suwen calls it fierce Qi of water and grain; the Lingshu says it warms flesh, fills skin, and works pores open and shut.

Traditional texts divide moisture into Jin and Ye. Jin is thin, keeping skin, lips, eyes, throat pliable. Ye is denser and goes deeper: joints, organs, marrow, brain. Dryness takes Jin first, though real bodies blur the chart. When the Lung cannot spread fluid outward, a quart of water can sit in the gut while the lips stay cracked. Distribution matters as much as volume. The old books favor warm liquids, steam, and damp air because movement, not sheer amount, is the work.

Two Kinds of Dryness

Late October is when the two kinds of dryness stop looking alike. Two people share a waiting room, both coughing. One is flushed and tugging at his collar; the other is bundled in a scarf, sipping warm water without thirst.

Cool dryness looks like the person in the scarf: more chilled than feverish, a nose dry enough to sting, and a cough that keeps trying to clear something that never arrives. The tongue coating is thin, white, and parched; the pulse sits near the surface. Wind-cold usually brings a runnier nose and looser phlegm.

The flushed person fits warm dryness, which may trail a hot summer. The heat is louder than the chill. They want cold water, and the tongue looks red. Wind-heat can look similar, but arrives quickly with a sore throat and thicker yellow phlegm; warm dryness builds slowly and keeps dryness at the center.

Wu Tang's Wen Bing Tiao Bian adopted Xing Su San (Apricot Kernel and Perilla Leaf Powder) from the earlier Yi Zong Jin Jian for cool dryness; his own Sang Xing Tang (Mulberry Leaf and Apricot Kernel Decoction) addressed early warm dryness. Neither is a shelf remedy. Bitter apricot kernel is toxic if poorly prepared or overused. Traditional home care favors warm fluids and steam; a persistent cough falls outside this seasonal description.

When Dryness Isn't the Story

A dry throat does not establish Yin deficiency. External dryness follows the weather; Yin deficiency does not. Yin deficiency may include night sweats, hot palms and soles, and a red tongue with little coating. Yang deficiency brings cold limbs, a wish for warmth, fatigue, a pale tongue, and a deep, weak pulse. Coughing blood, persistent fever, weight loss, or breathlessness falls outside this pattern.

Food and the Kitchen

At market I squeeze for Hachiyas that give like water balloons, then wait until they spoon out like honey custard. Persimmons are cold-natured: food for the flushed, warm dryness, not the person in the scarf. Firm tannins can bind into a hard stomach mass that can be very hard to pass. Traditional practice favors ripe, peeled fruit after a meal; green fruit, skins, and large amounts on an empty stomach are the trouble. Fuyu types stay crisp. Doctors flag higher risk after stomach surgery, slow emptying, or a previous bezoar.

In old kitchen practice, steaming blunts a pear's coolness while keeping its juice; ginger rides along for the scarf-wearer. Core it, add ginger, and cook until it slumps.

The tradition credits roasted chestnuts with tonifying Spleen and Kidney and strengthening sinew; they are sweet and warm. White radish, Luo Bo, sends Qi downward and loosens phlegm.

The old caution that radish may counter ginseng is real but narrow: it usually concerns concentrated radish seed, Lai Fu Zi, not the slices in soup.

By month's end, the woodpile puddle may not thaw. Ollie will tuck in earlier, one foot drawn up into his feathers before full dark. I'll keep a pear and ginger on the counter, and the kettle warm.
