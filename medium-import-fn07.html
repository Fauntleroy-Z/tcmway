Don't Zip Up Yet: The Old Chinese Rule for Autumn

COMICMARKERFN07

A sharp mid-September breeze slips through the screen door, dropping the porch thermometer right down to 14 degrees Celsius. Instinct kicks in immediately. Your hand reaches toward the entryway coat rack for the thick fleece, ready to seal out the cold. Before you can pull the zipper up, an older voice calls out from the kitchen table: "Not yet."

Perched on the windowsill nearby, Ollie, our resident screech owl, blinks in quiet agreement. His small jade yin-yang pendant catches the morning light, tapping lightly against the sill.

This seasonal hesitation comes from a four-character proverb known across China: chun wu qiu dong. It translates to "in spring, keep covered; in autumn, stay a little cold."

A companion saying offers the same advice from another angle: qiu bu mang tian yi, meaning "in autumn, do not hurry to add clothes."

Step outside in early fall and you will see the practical result: er ba yue luan chuan yi, or "in the second and eighth months, everyone dresses at random." Down parkas walk alongside shorts and T-shirts on the very same sidewalk. Crisp mornings demand two hands around a mug of hot tea, but by early afternoon, the sun still throws late-summer heat onto the street.

Why wait to zip up?

In classical thought, summer draws the body's Qi outward toward the skin. Autumn reverses that flow, pulling Yang inward to protect reserves for the winter ahead. The Suwen (Basic Questions), one of the oldest classical medical texts, describes autumn as rong ping, a state of being "calm and settled."

The text encourages retiring earlier, keeping the mind quiet, and practicing shou lian shen qi, or gathering inward.

None of this is about proving toughness. Folk wisdom turned those classical guidelines into daily laundry choices. If you bundle up in heavy layers at the sight of the first yellow leaf, your skin stays hot and traps perspiration.

That leads directly to a classical worry: han chu dang feng, or sweating then meeting a draft.

Walk ten minutes in a coat you do not need, break a light sweat, and damp skin becomes an open door for brisk winds. Autumn is a time to gather and settle, not remain stuck in July humidity.

"There is a grand difference," Ollie likes to note from his perch, "between letting a brisk breeze wake you up and shivering on a train platform to prove a philosophical point."

The rule was never an endurance contest. The grandmothers who quote the proverb are the same ones tucking extra wool scarves into your bag for good measure.

The tradition built clear boundaries around the idea. Infants, the elderly, frail people, and anyone managing chronic health issues were never expected to practice qiu dong. They should dress warmly the moment the weather shifts.

The same exception applies to anyone dealing with yang xu (yang deficiency)—the pattern where someone runs cold year-round, struggles with icy hands, and rarely feels warm. Nobody is asked to shiver or catch a chill for the sake of an idiom.

Six months later, the mirror rule arrives: chun wu. When early spring brings a sudden mild afternoon, you keep your layers on longer than you think you need them.

Taken together, the two rules form a buffer between the body and the weather outside. They suggest lagging a step behind the shifting climate rather than rushing ahead of the calendar.

The instinct is not unique to Chinese culture. Nordic parents leave babies napping outside in strollers during brisk afternoons.

Give yourself a beat before reaching for the heaviest wool in the closet. Let the new season settle in on its own terms.
